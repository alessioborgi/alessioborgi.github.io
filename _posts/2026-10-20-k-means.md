<div class="tldr-box">
  <strong>TL;DR:</strong> K-means chooses k centres to minimise squared Euclidean reconstruction error. Lloyd’s algorithm alternates nearest-centre assignments with cluster means. Each exact step improves or preserves the objective, but the result depends on initialisation and on whether Euclidean distance describes the structure you want.
</div>

## Start with the representation

Suppose each customer has two features: monthly spending and number of visits. A centre represents a typical feature vector, and each customer is assigned to the nearest representative. This is a compression problem before it is a discovery problem: replace many vectors with a small codebook while keeping squared error small.

Spending measured in cents rather than pounds changes the geometry unless the features are scaled. Standardisation is one possible modelling choice, not a universal correction. If monetary differences genuinely matter more than visit counts, equalising their variances may remove that preference. Decide what distance means before selecting k.

## The objective and the two updates

For vectors $$x_i\in\mathbb R^d$$, assignments $$z_i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$$ and centres $$\mu_j$$, minimise

<div class="formula-box">
\[
J(z,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^n\|x_i-\mu_{z_i}\|_2^2.
\]\n</div>

With centres fixed, each observation contributes independently. Choose the centre with the smallest squared distance. With assignments fixed, differentiate the cost of one nonempty cluster:

<div class="formula-box">
\[
\frac{\partial J_j}{\partial\mu_j}=2|C_j|\mu_j-2\sum_{i\in C_j}x_i=0,
\qquad \mu_j=\frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{i\in C_j}x_i.
\]\n</div>

The arithmetic mean is therefore the exact minimiser for that assignment. It is not an interchangeable choice of representative: changing to an absolute-distance objective leads toward medians or medoids, not the same update.

Lloyd’s algorithm repeats these two conditional minimisations. Under a consistent tie rule and appropriate handling of empty clusters it terminates at a stable partition, or an implementation stops at a tolerance. Monotonic improvement does not imply the globally best partition. A centre can never reach another arrangement if doing so requires an initially worse assignment.

## Work through a full iteration

Take the one-dimensional data $$0,2,3,9,10$$ and initialise centres at 0 and 3. The initial assignments are $$\{0\}$$ and $$\{2,3,9,10\}$$. Their centres update to 0 and 6, reducing the objective from 86 to 50.

Reassigning with a tie sent to the first centre gives $$\{0,2,3\}$$ and $$\{9,10\}$$. The updated centres are $$5/3$$ and $$9.5$$. Their squared-error contributions are $$14/3$$ and $$1/2$$, for a total of $$31/6\approx5.167$$. Another assignment step changes nothing.

```python
points = [0.0, 2.0, 3.0, 9.0, 10.0]
centres = [0.0, 3.0]
objectives = []
for _ in range(10):
    labels = [min(range(2), key=lambda j: (x-centres[j])**2)
              for x in points]
    groups = [[x for x, z in zip(points, labels) if z == j]
              for j in range(2)]
    assert all(groups)  # This example never creates an empty cluster.
    updated = [sum(g)/len(g) for g in groups]
    objectives.append(sum((x-updated[z])**2
                          for x, z in zip(points, labels)))
    if updated == centres:
        break
    centres = updated
assert all(b <= a + 1e-12 for a, b in zip(objectives, objectives[1:]))
assert abs(objectives[-1] - 31/6) < 1e-10
```

This deliberately small implementation exposes the mechanism. A production implementation also needs an empty-cluster policy, iteration limits, efficient distance computation and reproducible initialisation.

## Why k-means++ helps

Choose the first centre from the observations. For each later centre, let $$D_i$$ be the distance to the closest already selected centre, and sample observation i with probability proportional to $$D_i^2$$. If candidate squared distances are 1, 4 and 25, their probabilities are $$1/30,4/30,25/30$$.

This spreads initial representatives across regions that are currently badly represented. The original k-means++ analysis bounds expected seeded objective relative to the optimum; it does not promise that every run discovers a scientifically meaningful grouping. Multiple initialisations remain useful. Report the restart count explicitly rather than relying on a library default.

## What the geometry permits

Nearest-centre boundaries are hyperplanes, giving convex Voronoi cells. K-means can quantise an elongated cloud, but it may divide it across its long axis rather than preserve a domain-defined group. Two concentric rings cannot be represented as two ordinary nearest-centroid regions. More restarts cannot repair that mismatch.

Squared distance also gives faraway points large influence. Adding a single extreme customer can move a mean substantially or consume a centre. [K-medoids](/blog/basics/k-medoids/) changes the representative and loss; [DBSCAN](/blog/basics/dbscan/) changes the definition of a cluster and can leave observations unassigned.

## Choosing k and validating the result

The globally optimal training objective cannot increase as k grows. Therefore choosing the smallest training error selects as many centres as allowed. An elbow is a heuristic, and separate local optimisation runs may even produce small violations of the expected decreasing pattern.

Use [clustering evaluation](/blog/basics/clustering-evaluation/) to examine stability, silhouettes, representative observations and domain utility. If the goal is compression, held-out quantisation error is relevant. If the goal is customer segmentation, verify that the segments support a useful decision and persist under plausible data changes.

A full assignment pass costs roughly $$O(nkd)$$; T passes cost $$O(Tnkd)$$ before acceleration. [Mini-batch k-means](/blog/basics/mini-batch-k-means/) trades exact full-data updates for cheaper stochastic ones. Record feature transformations, centres, random seed, objective and cluster sizes so another person can reproduce the partition.

<div class="insight-box">
  <strong>Key Insight:</strong> The centroid update is an exact least-squares solution inside an otherwise nonconvex problem. Understanding which part is exact explains both Lloyd’s reliable descent and its dependence on the starting centres.
</div>

<div class="warning-box">
  <strong>Common pitfall:</strong> A small inertia proves only that the chosen representation is well approximated by k centres. It does not establish the existence of k natural populations.
</div>

  <h3>Recap</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Derive nearest-centre assignments and mean updates from the same objective.</li>
    <li>Use explicit initialisation, restarts and empty-cluster handling.</li>
    <li>Inspect scale, outliers and convex-cell assumptions before interpreting groups.</li>
    <li>Evaluate k using the intended task and stability, not training inertia alone.</li>
