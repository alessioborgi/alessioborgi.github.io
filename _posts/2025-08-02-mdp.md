<div class="tldr-box"><strong>TL;DR:</strong> A Markov decision process specifies states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and an objective. A state is sufficient for predicting the next transition given the action. What a sensor observes may contain only part of that information.</div>

## A photograph may not be a state

**Intuition first.** Two images show a ball at the same height. In one case it is rising; in the other it is falling. Position alone does not predict where it will be next. Adding velocity makes a better state description for this simple physical system.

A state is a modelling choice about the information needed for the future. An observation is the information delivered to the agent. A camera frame, noisy measurement, or partial map need not be Markov.

## Specify the decision process

For a finite MDP, use $$\mathcal S$$ for states, $$\mathcal A$$ for actions, and $$P(s'\mid s,a)$$ for the probability of the next state. Let $$r(s,a,s')$$ be the expected reward on that transition and $$\pi(a\mid s)$$ the policy's action probability.

The Markov condition says that, given the state and action, earlier history adds no predictive information about the next state:

<div class="formula-box">
\[
P(S_{t+1}\mid S_t,A_t,\text{history})
=P(S_{t+1}\mid S_t,A_t).
\]
</div>

A full task also specifies how episodes start and end. For a fixed-duration task, remaining time may need to be included in the state because the best action can depend on the deadline. [MDP lecture](https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-2-mdp.pdf), [finite-horizon semantics](https://gymnasium.farama.org/tutorials/gymnasium_basics/handling_time_limits/).

## Worked example: put reward on the transition

Consider a deterministic chain with one action per state:

| Transition | Reward after the action |
|---|---:|
| A to B | 0 |
| B to Goal | 1 |

Goal is terminal: no more reward follows it. With $$\gamma=0.9$$,

<div class="formula-box">
\[
V(\mathrm{Goal})=0,\qquad V(B)=1,\qquad V(A)=0.9.
\]
</div>

Starting at B receives the reward immediately after its first action, so it is not discounted. Starting at A receives that same reward after one intervening step, so it gets one factor of $$\gamma$$. Giving B value 0.9 would insert a step that is absent from the defined task.

Writing down transitions and rewards before calculating values prevents this common indexing error.

## Transitions and policies are different distributions

Suppose “move right” succeeds with probability 0.8 and otherwise leaves the agent in place. That uncertainty belongs to $$P$$. If the policy chooses right with probability 0.6, that uncertainty belongs to $$\pi$$.

In this toy example, the probability of both selecting right and moving successfully is $$0.6\times0.8=0.48$$. Even a deterministic policy may produce varied trajectories because the environment is stochastic.

## What to do with incomplete observations

A sequence of recent measurements can reveal velocity that one image hides. A recurrent policy can maintain a memory, while a state estimator can maintain a distribution over hidden states. Neither approach magically makes the problem fully observed; its effectiveness depends on the available evidence.

For a robot, also distinguish a physical state from a simulator's privileged state. A training policy that sees exact object positions must have an explicit plan for obtaining equivalent information at deployment.

<div class="insight-box"><strong>Key Insight:</strong> The MDP specifies the problem before specifying the algorithm. State information, transition timing, terminal conditions, and reward placement determine what the agent is actually learning.</div>
